# A careful hand, a box in the cable, and a recorder gone blind

*4 November 2026. Numbers and code: [research/results/2026-11-04-delivery](../results/2026-11-04-delivery/README.md).*

Three questions were left open by the earlier articles. Each is about how
input reaches the machine rather than who types it: a remote operator who
knows about the burst signal, a small device wired between my keyboard and
the machine, and a tool that takes my keyboard away from the recorder.

## A careful remote operator

[A remote hand](2026-11-04-remote-hand.md) showed that a long network path
lands whole packets of keys at once, near-simultaneous events that my own
keyboard almost never produces. I asked what is left when the operator
smooths that away. Two ways to do it:

- **Spread each packet over a frame**, the obvious fix. It works on the burst
  signal: from 0.04 on one repetition, the detection error rises to 0.47.
  But the timing it delivers is still the path's, not the typist's: my own
  template notices my typing came through it (0.06), and another person is
  accepted as me 0.2 % of the time, against 9.6 % when typing locally. The
  path destroys identity in both directions. Spreading also lays events on
  one even step, so exactly repeated gaps give it away (0.20 over five
  repetitions).
- **A jitter buffer**: stamp each event at the sender, replay it a fixed
  250 ms later. This is the careful operator. Bursts and repeated gaps fall to
  chance, my template no longer sees the path (0.47), and 2.8 % of events
  arriving late leave no usable trace.

So the answer is the honest one: a careful operator with a jitter buffer is
invisible in the timing. What remains is that they are not me. Through the
buffer, an impostor is accepted 9.0 % of the time, the same as typing at my
desk. E11 catches the careless operator; against the careful one, identity
is the whole defence, together with what the shell can say about an open
remote session (E21).

## A box in the cable

[The thief article](2026-11-04-stolen-rhythm.md) found that a stolen template
forges me 96 % of the time against the classic detector, and noted that a
forger still has to emit the timings. A device between my keyboard and the
machine is the hard case: real hardware, no virtual device (E01), nothing
plugged in while I work (E19).

But it has to report on a USB polling grid, and so does my keyboard. On my
own machine the grid is plainly visible in the timestamps the recorder
reads: my mouse reports every 8 ms, and its intervals align at 0.998 on a
1 ms grid. On CMU, which carries no grid of its own, I put my keyboard on a
1 ms or an 8 ms grid and let the forger emit through a rig on either:

| My keyboard | Rig | Grid test error |
|---|---|---|
| 1 ms | 8 ms | 0.00 |
| 8 ms | 1 ms | 0.00 |
| 1 ms | 1 ms | 0.50 |
| 8 ms | 8 ms | 0.50 |

A rig on the wrong grid is caught on a single repetition. A rig on the right
grid is invisible, and the quantisation costs the forger nothing (96 % to
95 %). Software that emits perfect timings is caught at once, because no
real keyboard does that, which is a second reason, after E01, that injected
input stands out.

So the grid test is worth having, because it costs nothing and catches a lazy
rig. A careful rig matches my keyboard's rate, which a USB descriptor makes
easy. The lasting answer is to know my devices: my keyboard's polling rate
and identity become part of the enrolment, and a change in either is an
event, not a detail.

## A recorder gone blind

The [kloak study](2026-11-04-kloak.md) found that a tool grabbing my keyboard
(`EVIOCGRAB`) takes every event for itself, and the recorder simply stops
seeing keys. If the tool re-injects through `uinput`, E01 sees a virtual
device. If it re-injects through the compositor, or sends the keys
elsewhere, the recorder goes quiet while I keep typing. Phantom activity
(E20) caught that only when focus changed.

The compositor knows better. GNOME's idle monitor resets on any input it
handles, so its idle time says when it last saw input. When that is more than
two seconds after the last event the recorder got, three times in a minute,
something reaches my desktop that never reaches my devices. That is the new
signal, E23. It reads the session bus every five seconds, needs no extension,
and turns the bar red like E20: it says nothing about who types, only that I
cannot see. Input from a device I do not open (a touchscreen, a pen, an
on-screen keyboard) looks the same, which is why it takes several misses and
is worded as input I cannot see rather than as an attacker.

## Limits

The remote paths are a model, not measured links. The grid test is simulated
on CMU, and real USB stacks add structure of their own. E23 is unit-tested,
not yet measured over days of my own use; its thresholds are a starting
point.
